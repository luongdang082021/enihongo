## What Katakana is for

Katakana has the **same sounds** as Hiragana: the same 46 basic letters, counted in beats. Only the shapes differ. Japanese uses Katakana mainly for:

- **Loanwords** from other languages, above all English: コーヒー (coffee), ホテル (hotel), テスト (test).
- **Foreign names**, for both places and people.
- **Sound effects**, and **emphasis**, much like capitals or italics.
- Names of animals and plants in scientific writing.

So learning Katakana lets you read menus, signs and messages before you know much Japanese: many loanwords sound close to English.

## How it differs from Hiragana

Hiragana has **round, soft** strokes. Katakana has **straight, angular** strokes, usually fewer of them.

| Sound | Hiragana | Katakana |
|---|---|---|
| a | あ | ア |
| ka | か | カ |
| ki | き | キ |
| ri | り | リ |
| n | ん | ン |

Some pairs look alike (か and カ, り and リ), but many look completely different (あ and ア). Do not try to match letters one by one; learn Katakana as a script of its own.

## How the course works

- **Lessons 2 to 7** teach the basic chart row by row, as in Hiragana. Example words are familiar loanwords.
- **Lesson 8** collects the most confusing pairs (シ and ツ, ソ and ン) for focused practice.
- **Lessons 9 to 11** teach voiced sounds, combined sounds, the long-vowel mark **ー**, small **ッ** and extended sounds such as ファ and ティ.
- **Lesson 12** reviews everything with common loanwords.

We suggest learning Hiragana first, but it is not required: romaji and the idea of beats work exactly the same way.

## One real difference

Hiragana writes long vowels by repeating the vowel (おかあさん). Katakana uses a dedicated mark, **ー**: コーヒー is read *koohii*. You will learn it in lesson 10.
